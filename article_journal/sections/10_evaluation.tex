\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This evaluation asks whether the benchmark, the observation boundary, the referee
and the team scoring represent and expose differences between autonomous
behaviours under common experimental conditions. We organize the experiments
around five research questions.

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] Can \mra{} support fully onboard autonomous completion across
heterogeneous underwater racing circuits?
\item[RQ2] Does the benchmark expose condition-dependent differences between
autonomous control strategies?
\item[RQ3] How do environmental currents affect benchmark performance?
\item[RQ4] Can the same evaluation framework support multi-vehicle and
team-level racing?
\item[RQ5] Can learning-based controllers be integrated and evaluated within the
same participant-level information boundary and referee procedure?
\end{description}

\subsection{Protocol and Metrics}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

The systematic benchmark evaluation comprises both reference controllers on the
three clean circuits, the \texttt{medium} current condition on Horseshoe Bay,
homogeneous two-vehicle fleets and three-vehicle coordination trials, for $68$
runs in total. These experiments run at $30$\,Hz. The current-free completion
demonstration comprises
nine runs covering the three official circuits, without current, at $10$\,Hz.
The recurrent PPO validation uses three episodes per circuit at $10$\,Hz.

The $10$\,Hz current-free validation and the $30$\,Hz systematic benchmark are
separate experimental protocols that ran at different control rates, and their
completion times are therefore not comparable; the difference between a
$10$\,Hz table and a $30$\,Hz table is not attributed here to any single cause.
Every timing comparison below is made within a single protocol.

All cells use three to five seeds and are reported descriptively. No statistical
test is performed anywhere in this section, and no difference reported below
should be read as inferential. The two rule-based controllers are reference
implementations included to exercise the benchmark, not a selection of
state-of-the-art methods, and the purpose of the comparisons that follow is to
characterize what the benchmark records rather than to establish the superiority
of one controller over the other.

All experiments use the native HoloOcean backend with a BlueROV2-class vehicle
and participant-accessible onboard observations. The reported metrics are
completion, completed gates, official and penalized time, collisions,
out-of-bounds, wrong-direction and stuck events; fleet trials additionally report
inter-vehicle proximity and both elapsed and penalized team time.

\subsection{RQ1: Onboard-Only Completion Across Heterogeneous Circuits}
\label{sec:eval_rq1}

The first question is whether the task is solvable under its own restrictions.
RQ1 therefore tests end-to-end completion using only onboard sensing.

Table~\ref{tab:current_free} answers this affirmatively for the current-free
setting. The Center-then-Commit controller completed all
three official circuits in every one of nine runs: 3/3 on Horseshoe Bay (12
gates), 3/3 on Vertical Serpent (17 gates) and 3/3 on Mixed Endurance (22 gates),
for 51 of 51 gates in total. Across those nine runs the referee recorded zero
gate, bar or world collisions, zero out-of-bounds events and zero
wrong-direction crossings, and penalized time equalled official time in every
run because no penalty was ever charged.

\input{tables/current_free}

Completion time scales with course length roughly as expected from the track
geometry: $236.0\pm6.9$\,s over $93.8$\,m, $308.6\pm6.5$\,s over $118.3$\,m and
$480.4\pm15.7$\,s over $206.3$\,m, i.e.\ approximately $0.40$, $0.38$ and
$0.43$ course metres per second of elapsed time. These figures are ratios of
course length to completion time and are not vehicle speeds: the length is
measured along the nominal course rather than along the path actually travelled,
and the time includes every hold, re-acquisition and alignment manoeuvre.
Similar descriptive progress rates were observed on three structurally different
circuits --- planar, vertically serpentine and long mixed. Whether that
similarity holds beyond these nine runs is not tested here.

In these runs the task was completed end to end under the full participant-level
observation boundary on three structurally different circuits. All nine runs use
the $10$\,Hz protocol, so their times are not comparable with those of the
$30$\,Hz tables in the remainder of this section.

\subsection{RQ2: Condition-Dependent Differences Between Reference Controllers}
\label{sec:eval_rq2}

The question here is what the benchmark records when the same course is
traversed by two differently designed controllers, not which of the two is
better. The two reference controllers of Section~\ref{sec:controller} share the
same observations, guidance, tracker and passage-verification logic, and differ
by a documented branch in the \textsc{commit} and \textsc{verify\_exit} phases.
Both remain reference implementations rather than tuned or state-of-the-art
methods, and the five seeds per cell reported below are described rather than
tested for significance.

\input{tables/clean_tracks}
\input{figures/controller_comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:clean_tracks} and Fig.~\ref{fig:controller_comparison} report a
condition-dependent picture: neither controller ranks ahead of the other on all
three circuits. On the short, largely planar Horseshoe Bay circuit the two
records are identical on every discrete metric: both complete all $12$ gates in
all five seeds with no collision, out-of-bounds or stuck event.
Center-then-Commit's mean time is $3.2$\,s higher ($197.7\pm7.1$\,s versus
$194.5\pm4.8$\,s), a gap smaller than the seed-to-seed spread of either
controller, with no difference in recorded safety events. On this circuit the
two traversal strategies are not separated by any recorded metric.

The two records differ on the harder circuits, and they differ in opposite
directions. On Vertical Serpent, Continuous Servo finished $5/5$ against $4/5$
and completed $17.0$ against $14.8$ mean gates. On Mixed
Endurance, which is more than twice as long, the ordering is reversed:
Center-then-Commit finishes all five seeds and completes every gate
($22.0/22$), while Continuous Servo finishes only $2/5$ and averages $17.0/22$.

The design argument of Section~\ref{sec:center_commit_baseline} offers one
reading of this pattern, which the present runs do not test: continuous visual
correction may matter little when a passage is short and the approach is well
conditioned, while partial near-field contours could move the image centroid at
the point where the aperture margin is smallest, and a longer course offers more
passages at which that can occur. Distinguishing this account from other
explanations would require instrumented per-passage measurements that these runs
do not provide. That Continuous Servo nonetheless records the better result on
Vertical Serpent while recording the worse one on Mixed Endurance means the
ordering is not one-directional: the evaluation exposes different
condition-dependent behaviours of the two reference strategies, which is
precisely what a single-circuit benchmark would have concealed.

\subsection{RQ3: Behaviour Under Environmental Currents}
\label{sec:eval_rq3}

Table~\ref{tab:currents} reports the \texttt{medium} current condition on
Horseshoe Bay. The columns of that table are conditioned differently, and the
distinction matters for how they are read: completion and collision counts are
computed over all five runs of each cell, whereas official and penalized times
are computed over the completed runs only. Under this condition,
Continuous Servo finishes $2/5$ runs, averages $8.4/12$ completed gates and
$43.4$ gate and world collisions per run, with official and penalized times of
$337.9\pm19.2$\,s and $692.9\pm75.7$\,s. Center-then-Commit
finishes $3/5$, averages $8.8/12$ gates and $25.2$ collisions, with official and
penalized times of $217.3\pm1.5$\,s and $223.9\pm1.8$\,s. The time cells of
Continuous Servo therefore average its two completed runs and those of
Center-then-Commit its three, over different subsets of seeds, so the two rows
are not comparable on equal terms.

\input{tables/currents}

The relevant contrast for benchmark design is between
Table~\ref{tab:clean_tracks} and Table~\ref{tab:currents}. Both controllers
record $5/5$ with zero events on clean Horseshoe Bay, and $2/5$ and $3/5$ with
tens of collisions on the same circuit under a moderate current. In these runs
clean-track success did not predict behaviour under current, and a protocol
reporting only clean completion would not have exposed this difference. The
medium-current condition thus gives the benchmark an operating point at which
both controllers' recorded behaviour differs from the clean case: fewer
completions, and collisions where the clean condition recorded none.

The difference between the two controllers under current --- $43.4$ against
$25.2$ collisions per run, and $2/5$ against $3/5$ completions --- is recorded
here as a descriptive observation over five seeds. It is secondary to the
clean-versus-current contrast, which is the result relevant to benchmark design,
and one completion apart with no statistical test does not support a claim that
either controller is intrinsically the more robust of the two.

\subsection{RQ4: Multi-Vehicle and Team-Level Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval_rq4}

\input{tables/fleet}

The official fleet result is the penalized team time of \eqref{eq:team_pen},
which adds every per-vehicle penalty and the team-level inter-vehicle term to
the elapsed span of \eqref{eq:team_time}. Table~\ref{tab:fleet} reports both, and
the discussion below reads the penalized column as the score and the elapsed
column as a diagnostic showing how much of that score comes from charged
penalties rather than from time on the course.

The upper block of Table~\ref{tab:fleet} reports the two-vehicle homogeneous
case. With either controller, a homogeneous two-vehicle fleet on clean Horseshoe
Bay at a $90$\,s release gap finishes $24/24$ team gates in all five seeds, with
no gate or world collision, no inter-vehicle proximity event, no out-of-bounds
and no stuck event. No penalty is charged, so team elapsed and penalized times
coincide at $303.1\pm3.2$\,s for Continuous Servo and $308.0\pm5.8$\,s for
Center-then-Commit. This is deliberately a low-contact case: it exercises
multi-vehicle spawning, staggered release, independent per-vehicle referee
state, ranking and team aggregation without the vehicles interacting, since the
per-vehicle behaviour matches the single-vehicle case.

The interacting case is the one the team score is designed for. A heterogeneous
three-vehicle convoy --- a Continuous Servo leader followed by two
Center-then-Commit vehicles --- records substantially more contact. At an $8$\,s
gap the uncoordinated convoy finishes only $2/3$ runs, averages $34.3/36$ team
gates, $127.3$ gate and world collisions and $2.0$ inter-vehicle proximity events
per run. At simultaneous release it still finishes $3/3$, but averages $9.3$
collisions and $2.3$ proximity events. With the distributed leader--follower
policy of \eqref{eq:yield} enabled, no such events were recorded in these runs:
at both gaps LF(1) finishes $3/3$ with $36.0/36$ team gates and zero gate,
world, proximity, out-of-bounds and stuck events, and LF(2) likewise records no
contact, with one stuck-timer charge per run at simultaneous release.

The referee charges those events, so the penalized figures depart from the
elapsed ones by different amounts for the three policies. At simultaneous
release (gap $0$\,s) the uncoordinated convoy has the lowest elapsed time of the
three policies, $219.7\pm2.5$\,s, but a penalized team time of
$266.4\pm64.1$\,s; LF(1) is charged nothing and records $262.3\pm7.7$\,s on
both; LF(2) records $288.3\pm1.3$\,s elapsed and $303.3\pm1.3$\,s penalized.
At an $8$\,s gap the
uncoordinated convoy records $254.0\pm24.5$\,s elapsed and $564.0\pm463.0$\,s
penalized, both computed over the two of the three runs in which every vehicle
finished, since \eqref{eq:team_finished} defines a team result only when all
vehicles finish. LF(1) records $266.0\pm8.3$\,s and LF(2) $285.7\pm3.8$\,s at
that gap, with penalized equal to elapsed in both cases.

These figures are read on the score the benchmark awards rather than on elapsed
time. At simultaneous release the penalized team times of LF(1) and the
uncoordinated convoy are $262.3$\,s and $266.4$\,s, the second carrying a
standard deviation of $64.1$\,s over its three runs, so the comparison is not
resolvable from these runs. At an $8$\,s gap the difference is large ---
$266.0$\,s against $564.0$\,s, with LF(1) completing $3/3$ runs against $2/3$
--- but it still rests on three runs per cell, two of which produced the
uncoordinated figure, and the standard deviation of $463.0$\,s that accompanies
it is nearly as large as the mean itself. Those spreads reflect runs that
accumulate very different numbers of charged events. With $n=3$ per cell and
outcomes of that spread, these numbers describe the runs reported here; they do
not rank the three policies.

In these conditions both coordination settings recorded zero contact events,
while the larger nominal gate separation of LF(2) recorded higher times and, at
simultaneous release, one stuck penalty per run. LF(1) is the configuration
shipped as the default, and LF(2) is retained as a conservative comparison.

Two properties of this result matter beyond the numbers. The coordination policy
consumes only the vehicle's own tracker state, a static predecessor assignment
and teammate-authored messages delivered over a channel with range-dependent
latency and loss; it never reads referee progress or a teammate's true position,
so the difference in recorded events arises inside the same information boundary
that constrains everything else. And the cost of coordination is visible in the
score rather than hidden: LF(2)'s stuck penalty is charged by the same independent
referee that charges a collision, so a policy that buys safety by waiting pays
for the waiting.

\subsection{RQ5: Learning-Based Control Under the Same Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_rq5}

\input{tables/learning}

The recurrent PPO controller was trained and evaluated on the official circuit
families, and was then run for three episodes on each of the three official
circuits using the same participant-level information boundary and independent
referee employed throughout the benchmark. The policy completed all nine
evaluation episodes: 3/3 on Horseshoe Bay, 3/3 on Vertical Serpent and 3/3 on
Mixed Endurance. Because training and evaluation share the same circuit
families, this is a descriptive integration demonstration rather than a test of
generalization to circuits the policy has not seen.

No out-of-bounds event was recorded in any of the nine runs. Horseshoe Bay and
Mixed Endurance were completed without collisions, while one collision event was
recorded on Vertical Serpent. In these nine episodes the learned controller
composed local gate-navigation behaviour into complete ordered circuits while
remaining entirely within the participant-side observation contract.

The sample is intentionally small, with three episodes per circuit, and is
reported descriptively. Together, the rule-based and PPO evaluations illustrate
that the benchmark interface is controller-agnostic: autonomy methods with
different internal designs can be evaluated through the same participant-level
information boundary, required body-frame command interface and independent
referee procedure. The interface does not prescribe controller internals; the
instantiations exercised in this work are two rule-based reference controllers
and one recurrent PPO controller.
