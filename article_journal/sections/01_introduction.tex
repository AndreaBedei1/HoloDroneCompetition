\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Autonomous racing has emerged as an effective research format for evaluating
perception, planning, control and real-time decision-making within a single
quantitative task under repeatable conditions~\cite{betz2022autonomous}. In
ground robotics, F1TENTH provides standardized vehicles, circuits, metrics and
baselines for continuous control and reinforcement learning~\cite{okelly2020f1tenth}.
At full scale, the Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing League (A2RL) extends this idea
to head-to-head racing near the limits of vehicle handling~\cite{hoffmann2026a2rl}.
In aerial robotics, gate-based competitions have driven the development of
high-speed perception and control pipelines~\cite{foehn2020alphapilot}. More
recently, learning-based systems have demonstrated autonomous drone-racing
performance at champion level~\cite{kaufmann2023swift}. Across these domains,
the value of racing is not limited to speed: a fixed task, explicit course
geometry, common rules and measurable outcomes provide a practical basis for
comparing, reproducing and improving complete autonomous systems.

Underwater robotics presents a natural but substantially different setting for
this type of system-level evaluation. Autonomous underwater vehicles operate
under sensing, communication and actuation constraints that do not arise in the
same form in ground or aerial racing. Satellite-based global positioning is
unavailable underwater, while inertial and Doppler-based navigation accumulates
uncertainty over time. Vision-based navigation is further constrained by limited
visibility, illumination variations and water conditions
~\cite{manzanilla2019uuvnavigation}. Acoustic sensing and communication can
extend the observable range, but introduce limited bandwidth, propagation delay
and measurement uncertainty~\cite{stojanovic2009underwater}. Persistent water
currents and strongly damped vehicle dynamics additionally affect how a vehicle
approaches, aligns with and crosses a gate. Underwater racing is therefore not
simply a ground or aerial racing task transferred to a marine simulator.

A growing ecosystem of underwater simulators already provides the physical and
sensor models required to develop autonomous systems. UUV Simulator introduced
a Gazebo-based environment for underwater vehicle dynamics and multi-robot
experimentation~\cite{manhas2016uuvsim}. HoloOcean provides Unreal Engine
environments together with simulated underwater sensing and multi-agent support
~\cite{potokar2022holoocean}. More recently, MarineGym has emphasized
high-throughput simulation for learning-based underwater control
~\cite{chu2025marinegym}. These platforms provide increasingly capable
simulation backends, but a simulator alone does not define a racing benchmark.
In particular, it does not necessarily specify the ordered task to be completed,
which information a controller is allowed to use, how gate crossings are
validated, how penalties and timeouts are applied, or how different participants
are scored under a common set of rules.

This distinction is especially important in simulation because information that
is useful for evaluating an experiment may also provide an unrealistic advantage
to the controller. Direct access to global vehicle pose, exact gate coordinates,
the true current field or official course progress can substantially simplify
navigation, although such information may not be available to a deployed
underwater vehicle. Conversely, using only the controller's internal estimate to
determine whether a gate has been crossed can hide perception, localization or
sequencing errors. A meaningful benchmark should therefore separate the
information available to autonomy from the information required for evaluation.
Controllers should operate from observations representing onboard sensing,
whereas privileged simulator state should remain confined to an independent
evaluation layer. This separation allows the benchmark to determine what
actually occurred without simplifying the autonomy problem itself.

This paper presents Marine Race Arena, a configurable HoloOcean benchmark for
autonomous underwater gate racing and team-level fleet evaluation
(Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). Marine Race Arena is implemented as a benchmark and
race-management layer above the simulator rather than as a replacement for the
simulation backend. Complete race scenarios are described through declarative
configurations defining the environment, ordered gates, vehicle and sensor
configuration, acoustic beacons, environmental currents, obstacles,
participants, starting conditions, timing rules and penalties. The reference
implementation and the experiments reported in this work use HoloOcean with a
BlueROV2-class vehicle~\cite{vonbenzon2022bluerov2}, while simulator-specific
sensing and actuation are isolated behind an adapter layer.

A central feature of Marine Race Arena is its controller-agnostic autonomy
interface. The benchmark does not prescribe how a vehicle should navigate the
course. A controller receives the observations exposed by the participant
interface and returns bounded body-frame commands for surge, sway, heave and
yaw. Its internal implementation can therefore be rule-based, optimization-
based, learning-based or follow another autonomy paradigm without requiring
changes to the race runner or evaluation logic. The reference implementation
includes two interpretable rule-based controllers and a recurrent
reinforcement-learning controller as examples of substantially different
approaches operating through the same interface. New strategies can consequently
be integrated by mapping their inputs and outputs to the benchmark contract
rather than by modifying the benchmark itself.

The autonomy interface is complemented by a configurable and independent
race-management system. An automated referee uses privileged simulator state to
validate ordered gate crossings, detect collisions and other rule violations,
apply penalties and timeouts, determine race termination and compute the
official results. The controller never receives referee-side state or official
course progress. Race rules and evaluation conditions are instead defined by the
scenario configuration, allowing different racing tasks to be constructed
without modifying the autonomy stack. Marine Race Arena therefore separates two
roles that are often intertwined in simulation: the controller decides how to
complete the task from the information available onboard, while the referee
independently decides whether the task has been completed according to the
configured race rules.

The same structure extends naturally to multi-vehicle experiments. Multiple
vehicles can operate simultaneously on the same course while retaining
independent controllers, local state and official progress. The framework
supports staggered or simultaneous starts, optional inter-vehicle communication,
proximity monitoring, distributed leader--follower coordination and team-level
scoring. Communication between vehicles is modeled through an acoustic-inspired
channel with configurable delay and packet loss. The race-management and referee
mechanisms remain unchanged, allowing single-vehicle and fleet experiments to be
defined and evaluated within the same framework.

The release includes three ready-to-use gate-racing circuits with different
geometric characteristics together with configurable currents, obstacles,
starting conditions and participant configurations. Structured event logs and
machine-readable race summaries record controller execution and official
referee outcomes. We use these capabilities to evaluate rule-based and
reinforcement-learning controllers, disturbance conditions and multi-vehicle
coordination in HoloOcean. The experiments are intended to demonstrate that
different autonomy strategies and race configurations can be executed and
evaluated through the same controller interface and the same independent scoring
mechanism, rather than to prescribe a single optimal solution to underwater
racing.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{A configurable benchmark for autonomous underwater gate racing.}
Marine Race Arena defines complete race scenarios through declarative
configurations covering tracks, ordered gates, vehicles, onboard sensors,
acoustic beacons, environmental currents, obstacles, participants, starting
conditions, timing, penalties and scoring. Three ready-to-use circuits are
provided, while the same configuration model supports custom racing scenarios.

\item \textbf{A controller-agnostic autonomy interface.}\par
Controllers interact with the benchmark through a common observation--action
contract and can implement rule-based, optimization-based, learning-based or
other autonomy strategies without modifying the race execution or evaluation
logic. The release provides rule-based and reinforcement-learning controllers as
reference implementations of this interface.

\item \textbf{A configurable race manager with an independent automated referee.}
Race conditions and rules are defined independently from the controller, while
privileged simulator state is restricted to the evaluation layer. The referee
validates ordered gate crossings, detects violations, applies penalties and
timeouts and computes official race results without exposing privileged progress
information to autonomy.

\item \textbf{Single-vehicle and team-level multi-vehicle evaluation within the
same framework.}
Marine Race Arena supports independent per-vehicle autonomy, staggered or
simultaneous starts, optional acoustic-inspired communication, proximity
monitoring, distributed leader--follower coordination and team-level scoring
while maintaining independent official progress for each participant.

\item \textbf{An experimental validation of the common benchmark interface.}
The framework is exercised in HoloOcean across different race circuits, current
conditions and multi-vehicle configurations, including both rule-based reference
controllers and a reinforcement-learning controller integrated through the same
observation and action interface. Structured outputs and automated validation
procedures support repeatable evaluation and extension with new controllers and
racing scenarios.

\end{enumerate}
